# Goats and Sheep (Small Ruminants): Health and Management in West Africa

Scope: West African Dwarf (WAD) goats and sheep, plus Yankasa, Uda and Balami sheep. Written as an original summary of the sources named in each section. This is decision support, not a diagnosis: a vet must confirm disease and dosing.

## Goat and Sheep: Peste des petits ruminants (PPR, goat plague)

Farmer words: goat dey cough, nose and eye dey run, mouth sore, dey stool water, anus dey stick with dung, plenty goats dey die, kid dem dey die.

PPR is a viral disease and, per the FAO/ILCA review of the humid tropics, probably the most important cause of death in West African small ruminants, especially goats. It is worse in young animals around weaning.
- Signs: sudden fever, loss of appetite, depression, watery then thick eye and nose discharge, sores on lips and gums, severe diarrhoea, pasted vent, rough coat, difficulty breathing. Pneumonia and worms often make it worse.
- Spread: close contact with sick animals, through nose and eye discharge, saliva and dung. It does not infect humans.
- No cure for the virus. Control is vaccination of the whole village flock, plus isolation of sick animals, movement control, and cleaning of pens. A decades-old Nigerian village study reported about 75% less mortality in vaccinated groups; treat this as indicative only.
- Reported death rates differ between sources. FAO's programme page gives up to 90% of a herd infected and 30 to 70% of sick animals dying; other teaching sources report higher losses in unvaccinated goat flocks. Losses can be very high.
- Action: separate sick animals, do not sell or move them, and report suspected PPR to the state veterinary office. Ask about vaccination.
(Sources: FAO/ILCA Wilson et al.; FAO PPR programme; Nigerian PPR field study. See SOURCES.md.)

## Goat and Sheep: Worms (Haemonchus, barber pole worm) and anaemia

Farmer words: goat dey thin, dey weak, eye and inside of eyelid dey white, jaw dey swell (bottle jaw), black stool, e no dey grow.

Haemonchus contortus is a blood-sucking stomach worm and a major worm problem in small ruminants.
- Signs: anaemia (pale eyelids and gums), weakness, weight loss, swelling under the jaw, dark stool, sometimes sudden death.
- Diagnosis: faecal egg count and packed cell volume (PCV) are accurate but need a lab. The FAMACHA eye-colour chart helps, but Chiejina et al. (2015) note it is of limited use where trypanosomosis also occurs, because trypanosomes cause anaemia too.
- Treat only animals that need it, not the whole herd on a routine. Routine blanket deworming speeds up drug resistance.
- Prevention: avoid overcrowding, avoid grazing very short or wet pasture, rotate grazing, cull animals that repeatedly carry heavy worm burdens, and raise protein feeding for weak animals.
- Heavy worm infections are common at the peak of the rainy season (prevalence 80 to 100% in WAD goats), but disease is mainly a problem in young animals and where grazing is crowded with no rotation.
(Sources: Chiejina, Behnke and Fakae 2015; US university extension guides on small-ruminant parasites. See SOURCES.md.)

## West African Dwarf goats: natural worm resistance

WAD goats in Nigeria resist Haemonchus better than most other goat breeds, and are also trypanotolerant. In one experiment about 83% of goats harboured under 1% of a large larval dose, and in field surveys about 80% had very low worm burdens. This resistance is not found in all goats, and may not be present in WAD goats from other regions or in crosses with Sahelian goats. So a thin, anaemic WAD goat still deserves a vet check: do not assume worms are not the cause, and do not assume they are.
(Source: Chiejina, Behnke and Fakae 2015, Parasite 22:7.)

## Goat and Sheep: Coccidiosis in kids and lambs

Farmer words: kid dey stool water, stool get blood, belly dey push out, kid no dey grow, dem dey weak.

Coccidia damage the gut lining and mostly hit young animals, and stress and crowding make it worse.
- Signs: diarrhoea (sometimes bloody or dark), dull and weak young animals, poor growth, dehydration.
- Prevention: clean, dry pens; avoid overcrowding; keep feeders and waterers off the floor so they are not fouled with dung; reduce stress at weaning.
- Treatment: coccidiostat medicines such as amprolium or sulfa drugs exist, but dose and choice must come from a vet or agro-vet, and product registration differs by country. Give fluids and keep the kid warm.
(Source: US university extension sources; adapt to local conditions with a vet. See SOURCES.md.)

## Goat and Sheep: Mange, ticks and crowded pens in the rainy season

A flock study of WAD sheep and goats at Nsukka (Nigeria) reported mange, ticks and stomach-worm disease, with average monthly mortality of about 15% from June onwards. The authors blamed overcrowded sheds in the warm wet months (May to September), ineffective use of acaricides, poor grazing management, and no formal deworming programme. Lesson: in the rainy season, lower the stocking density, keep housing dry and ventilated, treat for external parasites on a proper schedule under vet advice, and plan deworming around the worm peak. FAO/ILCA also lists regular dipping or spraying as part of a tested health package.
(Sources: Nsukka flock study; FAO/ILCA Wilson et al. Older study, indicative.)

## Goat and Sheep: Feeding with browse, crop residues and priority animals

- Cut-and-carry browse from legume trees such as Leucaena and Gliricidia works as a protein supplement or sole feed for penned animals. Trials showed better lamb weaning output with browse.
- Crop residues, household waste and cassava peels are major feeds, especially for owners without land.
- Give feeding priority to does and ewes in late pregnancy and lactation, and to young animals at weaning, because they are under the most nutritional stress.
- Extra feed before mating ("steaming-up") can increase litter size, especially in animals in poor to moderate condition.
- Better nutrition lowers disease susceptibility and deaths.
- Alley farming idea: keep about 75% of tree foliage as mulch for the soil; the rest gives roughly 1 tonne dry matter per hectare of feed.
(Source: FAO/ILCA Wilson, Reynolds and Armbruster, Management of sheep and goat flocks in the humid tropics of West Africa.)

## Goat and Sheep: Kid and lamb losses and a simple health calendar

Deaths are highest in the period before weaning and in goats more than sheep. A practical plan, to be confirmed with the local vet: vaccinate against PPR for the whole flock on the vet's schedule; clean and dry the kidding pen; make sure newborns get colostrum; check young animals weekly for diarrhoea and pale eyelids; keep records of births, deaths, treatments and weights so problems show early. For Nigerian sheep, the usual management list is castration, dehorning, deworming, spraying, vaccination and hoof trimming.
(Sources: FAO/ILCA Wilson et al.; Nigerian sheep and goat production reviews.)

## Sheep in Nigeria: breeds, feeding and lambing figures

- Four main Nigerian breeds: Balami and Uda (semi-arid north), Yankasa (found throughout the country), and West African Dwarf (south). Balami is favoured for stall-fed fattening.
- Feeding trials with rams: Digitaria smutsii hay as basal diet, with about 2% of body weight as concentrate, fattened rams over 12 weeks. Yankasa was the cheapest to raise; Balami gave the highest income over expenses in one 90-day trial.
- Older research-station lambing rates: Uda about 129%, Yankasa about 113%, with overall mortality of 14% and 6%; a Katsina farm recorded about 110% for Balami.
- Flushing (better feed around breeding) raised lamb crop by 20 to 30% in an Ethiopian sheep study, but no trial in Nigerian breeds was found, so this evidence comes from another country.
(Sources: Nigerian Journal of Animal Production and related studies; see SOURCES.md. Breed-level data are old.)
