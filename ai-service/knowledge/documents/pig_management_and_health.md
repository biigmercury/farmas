# Pigs: Management, Feeding and Health in the Tropics

Scope: smallholder and small-scale intensive pig keeping in Nigeria and the tropics. Original summary based on Agrodok 1 (Muys and Westenbrink, Agromisa, 5th ed. 2004) and Mutua and Dione (2021). Agrodok is dated; ignore any pesticide or drug it names that is banned today (for example DDT, lindane and BHC), and check current products with a vet.

## Pig: African swine fever (ASF)

Farmer words: pigs dey die sharp-sharp, fever, skin red or purple, e no dey chop, e dey vomit, blood from nose, back leg weak.

- Signs: rapidly rising fever; the pig may stay lively and eat at first; 36 to 48 hours before death it becomes restless, stops eating, loses use of back legs and lies down; reddish-blue skin (legs, ears, belly), vomiting, sometimes bloody nose discharge. Several pigs sicken and die together.
- There is no treatment and no vaccine. ASF is not the same as classical swine fever. Biosecurity is the only tool.
- If you suspect ASF: stop all pig movement and sales, do not sell sick pigs to traders, isolate, and report to the state veterinary office. Bury or burn carcasses; do not dump offal in the bush or feed it to pigs or dogs.
- In Nigeria, FAO and the Federal Ministry of Livestock Development have been training farmers and developing a National ASF Control Strategy (2025); outbreaks have been reported again in 2026.
(Sources: Agrodok 1; Mutua and Dione 2021; FAO Nigeria reports. See SOURCES.md.)

## Pig: Biosecurity measures that smallholders can use against ASF

From Mutua and Dione (2021), Frontiers in Veterinary Science 8:689811:
- Limit new pigs coming from other farms, markets and villages; quarantine new animals; avoid borrowing or sharing boars.
- Fence the farm; control people, dogs, cats, rodents and birds; use clothes and footwear only for the farm; keep traders, butchers and visitors out of pens. Pig traders and butchers going farm to farm are a major risk.
- Clean first, then disinfect: remove dirt from vehicles, tools, boots and pens, then apply disinfectant.
- Boil swill (kitchen waste) for about 30 minutes before feeding. Do not feed raw or undercooked pork or contaminated crops.
- Bury carcasses safely; do not sell panic stock; get movement permits where required.
- Report suspected cases quickly; ASF resembles other pig diseases and needs lab confirmation.
- Free-ranging pigs make all this harder. Quick action (within about 14 days of the first cases) can reduce deaths a great deal in models.
(Source: Mutua and Dione 2021.)

## Pig: Classical swine fever, erysipelas and pneumonia

- Classical (ordinary) swine fever: high fever (41 to 42 C), diarrhoea, unsteady walk, bruise-like skin spots, abortion in sows; piglets are pale, huddle, and may die. No treatment; prevention is vaccination, so ask the vet for the current vaccine and schedule.
- Swine erysipelas: often confused with swine fever; typically one or a few pigs ill, in hot weather, with pink skin patches, stiff gait, hoarse screech and constipation. A vet may treat with penicillin.
- Pneumonia: coughing, laboured breathing, slow growth; worse with overcrowding, draughts, dust and the rainy season. Keep pens dry and draught-free; a vet may give antibiotics, or treat for lungworm.
(Source: Agrodok 1, sections 7.5 and 7.6.)

## Pig: Worms, mange and lice

- Worms are among the most serious problems in free-range and semi-intensive pigs; more than 30 types affect the gut, and young piglets are most vulnerable. Prevention: rotate fields, keep pens clean, deworm sows about 1 week before farrowing and wash them, then keep them inside to farrow.
- Mange causes itching and scaly skin around legs, eyes, ears and neck; wash with soap and water, then treat with a product advised by a vet. Lice and sand fleas cause irritation, especially in piglets.
- Do not treat routinely without need: repeated treatments reduce natural resistance, and organisms can become resistant if medicines are used at wrong doses or too often. Always finish an antibiotic course, and do not slaughter or sell meat during withdrawal.
(Source: Agrodok 1, sections 7.3 and 7.4.)

## Pig: Piglet anaemia (iron deficiency) and baby piglet care

Farmer words: piglet dey pale, e no dey grow, e dey fat for neck, piglet dey tire.

- Piglets kept on concrete get serious anaemia within 2 to 3 weeks because sow milk lacks enough iron. They turn pale and growth drops.
- Prevention: put a shovel of clean iron-rich soil (soil that no pigs have used, so no worm eggs) in a corner of the pen daily from the first week, and/or inject iron within 0 to 3 days after birth if available (common in intensive units). Wood ash gives calcium and phosphorus, not iron.
- Keep piglets warm; trim tusk points only if they fight over teats; castrate males in the first two weeks if healthy.
(Source: Agrodok 1, sections 4.3, 4.6 and 7.7.)

## Pig: Breeding, farrowing and weaning figures

- A sow comes on heat for 2 to 3 days about every 3 weeks; check for heat again 3 weeks after mating. Gilts should come on heat at 6 to 8 months. Gestation is about 114 days.
- Sows come on heat again within about a week of weaning. Wean at about 8 weeks, taking the sow away rather than the piglets; the sow can suckle for 6 to 8 weeks without losing too much weight.
- Keep records: heat dates, service, farrowing dates, litter size, deaths and weights.
(Source: Agrodok 1, chapters 4 and 5.)

## Pig: Feeding by stage

- Protein: weaners need about 20% protein; after about 3 months, 13% is enough. Pregnant sows need extra protein late in pregnancy; lactating sows need plenty of energy feed and water.
- To reach 13% protein: mix 1 part beans (about 20%) with 2 parts cereals (about 10%). Tubers and roots are poor in protein and need protein supplements such as soy, groundnut cake, fish meal or milk products.
- Add minerals (about 0.3 kg per 10 kg of feed mixture).
- Cook soybeans; check groundnuts for mould; do not feed cottonseed, safflower seed or cabbage seed. Give lactating sows extra meal per piglet plus a base ration of about 1 kg. At 3 to 4 weeks, give piglets easily digested food such as watery porridge.
- Always provide clean water.
(Source: Agrodok 1, chapter 6.)

## Pig: Housing in hot climates

Design depends on climate, whether it is a breeding or fattening unit, and local materials. Use shade, a roof of leaves or corrugated iron, a sloping floor to drain urine, a dung area attached to each pen, a feeding trough and a wallowing or wetting area (cooling works through evaporation of water from the skin). A pen of 12 to 15 weaners or about 10 fatteners is a manageable group. Provide heating for newborn piglets and shade for adults. For heat stress in a pig: shade, and wet or cool the head with cold water.
(Source: Agrodok 1, chapter 3 and section 7.8; Nigerian pig production reviews.)

## Pig: Other diseases to know

Tetanus (rusty wound, no treatment), mastitis, anthrax (blood from body openings, burn do not bury), brucellosis (abortion; dispose of infected animals), and trypanosomiasis (tsetse areas make pig keeping very difficult). Mastitis and enteritis need a vet. If a pig has diarrhoea from feed change, withhold feed and give only clean water for one to two days, then restart gradually.
(Source: Agrodok 1.)
