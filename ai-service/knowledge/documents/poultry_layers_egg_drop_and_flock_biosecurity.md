# Poultry: Layers (Egg Drop) and Flock Biosecurity

Original summary of University of Kentucky extension publication ASC-192, "Why Have My Hens Stopped Laying?" (Jacob and Pescatore), plus a 2024 review of infectious poultry diseases in smallholder systems in Africa. Product names and doses are not given; a vet should confirm disease.

## Poultry: Layers egg drop, investigate in this order

Farmer words: egg dey drop, hens no dey lay, fewer eggs, soft shell, no shell, layers dey stop to lay.

1. Confirm it is real. Check whether hens are eating eggs, hiding them, or a predator or thief is taking them.
2. Age. Laying begins at 18 to 22 weeks, peaks 6 to 8 weeks later (90% or more is common in good laying strains), then slowly declines.
3. Feed and water. Hours without feed cause a drop in proportion to how long the hens were out; lack of water is worse (blue combs, birds crowding at drinkers).
4. Light. See the lighting section below.
5. Heat. Panting, less feed, more water, smaller eggs.
6. Ration. A new feed batch, scratch grains or kitchen scraps, or a wrong ingredient can unbalance the diet.
7. Feed spoiling. Mouldy feed with mycotoxins.
8. Parasites and disease.
(Source: University of Kentucky ASC-192. A review of the flock history is the first step.)

## Poultry: Layers lighting, heat, water and calcium

- Day length: once in lay, hens need about 14 hours of light a day; production falls and often stops when day length drops under 12 hours, which is why a flock may slow in the autumn. Supplement morning light to hold at least 14 hours (the guide suggests one 40 W lamp per 100 square feet of coop).
- Heat stress: feed intake, egg numbers, egg size and hatchability all fall. Provide shade, ventilation and plenty of cool water.
- Water: water is the most essential nutrient and hens react more strongly to lack of water than lack of feed.
- Calcium: shell is mostly calcium carbonate and calcium need rises about fourfold when laying starts. Too little calcium gives thin or shell-less eggs and fewer eggs; vitamin D3 is needed to absorb it. Do not feed a high-calcium layer ration to young pullets.
- Salt and protein: too little salt causes feather eating; too much causes wet droppings and kidney problems. Methionine is the amino acid most often short in layer rations.
- Mould: aflatoxin from mouldy corn lowers feed intake and thins shells; discard wet or mouldy feed.
- Some anticoccidial drugs affect layers (nicarbazin: shell-less eggs and loss of brown-egg colour; monensin on low-protein diets). Use only layer rations in layers.
(Source: University of Kentucky ASC-192.)

## Poultry: Layers parasites and diseases that cut egg numbers

- Mites and lice: nervous flock, weight loss; egg production may fall about 10% and up to 20% in heavy infestations. Worms: birds eat but lose weight.
- Diseases listed with signs: fowl pox (scab-like lesions on face and comb); coccidiosis (bloody droppings); infectious bronchitis (coughing, sneezing, soft or misshapen eggs, watery egg white); Newcastle disease (respiratory and nervous signs, twisted neck, higher deaths, big egg drop); avian influenza (swollen face, dark red spots on legs and combs, diarrhoea); Mycoplasma and infectious coryza (swollen face, nasal discharge); fowl cholera (sudden deaths, swollen wattles).
- If several birds are sick or dying as well as the drop, treat it as disease: isolate, restrict movement, and call a vet.
(Source: University of Kentucky ASC-192, tables 2 and 3.)

## Poultry: Smallholder flock biosecurity and disease picture in Africa

From a 2024 review (Grace and colleagues):
- Viral diseases (Newcastle disease, avian influenza) and bacterial diseases (colibacillosis, salmonellosis) cause most chicken losses. Newcastle vaccination is the most widely practised, but coverage is low (about 26 to 36% in Ethiopia, Uganda and Tanzania).
- Keep new or sick birds apart for about 2 weeks; avoid buying birds of unknown origin or from markets when outbreaks are common; wash hands and use footbaths; limit visitors; keep housing away from roads and standing water.
- Stack manure for at least 3 weeks; bury, compost or burn dead birds; never slaughter or sell birds from a flock that is dying of disease.
- Avian influenza: keep birds apart from waterfowl, use solid roofs and sides to block wild birds and their droppings. Vaccines are costly and poorly suited to smallholders.
- Gumboro and Marek's disease matter most in intensive flocks of exotic breeds; coccidiosis matters in deep-litter houses.
- Eggs and meat: discard cracked eggs, cook eggs and chicken thoroughly, avoid raw eggs (salmonella).
(Source: Grace et al. 2024, The public health importance and management of infectious poultry diseases in smallholder systems in Africa.)
