# Fish Diseases and Health (Tilapia and Catfish) in Africa

Original summary of FAO CIFA Technical Paper 31 (Paperna 1996), "Parasites, infections and diseases of fishes in Africa: an update", and African farm surveys. A fish health specialist should confirm any diagnosis.

## Fish: Bacterial infections (Aeromonas, streptococcosis, vibriosis) and what triggers them

Farmer words: fish get red spots, fish dey bleed, belly dey swell, eye dey push out, fin dey rot, fish dey swim round-round, plenty fish dey die.

- Many bacteria in the pond water become dangerous only when fish are stressed. Triggers listed by FAO: handling injury, poor feeding, poor water quality, overstocking, cold temperatures.
- Streptococcosis (tilapia): swollen belly with fluid, skin sores, bulging eyes, erratic or circling swimming. Outbreaks after stress can kill 50 to 60% of stock.
- Vibriosis (tilapia): bleeding at fin bases, lethargy, stiffness, purple gills; death within 48 to 72 hours after handling.
- Aeromonas hydrophila and other ubiquitous bacteria: death within 24 to 48 hours, red spots, eroded fins and skin sores, pale liver and swollen belly. Losses up to 98% have been reported in overwintering tilapia.
- Gill and fin rot (myxobacteria): rapid gill damage, white or yellow patches on head and back, fin rot. Linked to handling damage, high organic load, ammonia or nitrite, and crowding.
(Source: FAO CIFA Technical Paper 31, chapter 3.)

## Fish: Columnaris is not Aeromonas

Flavobacterium columnare (columnaris, causing gill and skin damage) is a different bacterium from Aeromonas (which causes septicaemia with red spots and belly swelling). The two are often confused. A Ugandan survey found Flavobacterium columnare, Aeromonas and Edwardsiella on tilapia and catfish farms, often in healthy-looking fish too.
(Source: Walakira et al. 2014, Uganda Journal of Agricultural Sciences.)

## Fish: Common parasites

Farmer words: fish dey scratch for wall, white spots on skin, fish rub body, gill dey pale, fish dey flash.

Reported on Ugandan tilapia and catfish farms: white-spot parasite (Ichthyophthirius, "ich"), Trichodina and Ichthyobodo (skin and gill protozoa), and gill and skin flukes (Cleidodiscus, Gyrodactylus). Typical signs are scratching or flashing, white spots, excess mucus and pale or damaged gills. Poor water quality and crowding make these worse. Diagnosis needs a microscope; a fisheries extension officer can do a skin and gill scrape.
(Sources: Walakira et al. 2014; FAO CIFA Technical Paper 31.)

## Fish: Prevention, and why antibiotics should not be the first answer

- FAO advises against routine blanket medicated feeding: it is wasteful, harms the pond's natural processes, and breeds resistant bacteria. Antibiotics can only help while fish are still eating, and sensitivity testing is needed.
- A Nigerian catfish study found common outbreaks, self-prescribed antibiotics, and Aeromonas carrying resistance genes. Do not tell farmers to guess antibiotics.
- Prevention that works: avoid overstocking and overfeeding; handle fish gently and as little as possible, avoid handling in cold or hot extremes; keep water quality good (see the water quality notes); keep ponds covered against birds; quarantine new fish; clean nets and tools.
- A Ugandan farm survey recorded disease outbreaks with survival of only 0 to 30% on some farms, especially in catfish hatcheries.
- FAO also recommends banning imports of seed, breeders and eggs from tilapia-producing countries to keep exotic diseases out of Africa.
(Sources: FAO CIFA Technical Paper 31, section 3.1.6; Walakira et al. 2014; Nigerian catfish study.)

## Fish: What to ask the farmer first

Ask: how many fish died and how fast; species and size; stocking density; recent feeding or feed change; recent handling, transport, rain, heat or cold; water colour and smell; dissolved oxygen readings if any; any red spots, belly swelling, gill colour, scratching or flashing. Then advise water-quality steps first and a fisheries officer or vet for diagnosis.
