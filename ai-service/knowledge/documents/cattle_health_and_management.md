# Cattle: Health and Management in West Africa

Scope: cattle such as Bunaji (White Fulani), N'Dama and crosses, in smallholder, agropastoral and feedlot settings. Original summary of the sources named in each section; a vet must confirm disease and dosing.

## Cattle: Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD)

Farmer words: cattle dey drool, mouth and leg get blisters, dem dey limp, dem no dey chop, tongue sore, teat sore.

- Signs: fever, blister-like sores in the mouth, nose, teats, between the claws and at the top of the hoof; drooling, lameness, tooth grinding. Deaths are low in adults but higher in calves.
- Spread: direct contact with sick animals, and contaminated secretions. African buffalo can maintain the virus.
- No cure. Care means wound cleaning and antibiotics for secondary infection, given by a vet. Inactivated vaccines exist, but protection between virus strains is limited and cold chain is a problem.
- Action: isolate affected animals, stop movement and trading, and report suspected FMD to the veterinary authority.
(Source: Mwacalimba et al. 2025, Animals 15(1):97.)

## Cattle: Contagious bovine pleuropneumonia (CBPP, lung sickness)

Farmer words: cattle dey cough, e dey breathe hard, fever, chest pain, thin calf, joints dey swell.

- Signs: pneumonia with fluid around the lungs. Acute cases have high fever and laboured breathing; chronic cases cough and lose condition. Young calves may have swollen joints. Some animals carry it without signs.
- Spread: breathing in droplets from coughing animals, mainly with close repeated contact; chronic carriers and cattle movements and gatherings spread it.
- Control: vaccines (T1/44 and T1sr) give variable protection, reported at 30 to 60%, and T1/44 can cause reactions. Antibiotics such as tylosin or oxytetracycline reduce signs but do not fully cure. Movement control matters, but is hard with pastoral herds.
- FAO and WOAH describe CBPP as essentially confined to Africa, reported in around 18 countries in 2019. Guidance on control is old (2003).
(Sources: Mwacalimba et al. 2025; FAO and WOAH CBPP documents. See SOURCES.md.)

## Cattle: Lumpy skin disease (LSD)

Farmer words: lumps dey show for cattle skin, skin get boil-like swelling, eye dey water, fever, leg dey swell.

- Signs: fever, watery eyes, nasal discharge, depression, then firm skin nodules about 1 to 7 cm that can ulcerate and leave permanent hide damage. Complications include eye damage, pneumonia and maggot infestation. Recovery can take months.
- Spread: mainly biting insects (peaks in warm, wet weather), also lesions, secretions, contaminated feed and water, and moving infected animals.
- No specific treatment: wound care, anti-inflammatories and antibiotics for secondary infection, by a vet. Control is early identification, quarantine, movement control and vaccination (live vaccines such as Neethling and KS-1 are widely used and can cause reactions).
- Action: isolate cattle with lumps, control flies, and report to the veterinary authority.
(Source: Mwacalimba et al. 2025; FAO LSD webinars.)

## Cattle: Rift Valley fever (RVF)

- Signs: "abortion storms" in pregnant animals and deaths of newborns; calves show fever, loss of appetite, diarrhoea and jaundice. Adults often show no signs. Deaths are usually under 10% in cattle but can reach 100% in newborn ruminants.
- Spread: mosquitoes; also direct contact with infected tissues, aborted material and fluids. Humans can be infected, so avoid handling abortions without gloves and protection.
- No specific treatment. Vaccination is used during outbreaks; vector control targets mosquito breeding sites.
(Source: Mwacalimba et al. 2025.)

## Cattle: Bovine respiratory disease (BRD) and new arrivals

- Signs: high fever, depression, nose and eye discharge, coughing, fast or laboured breathing.
- Triggers: stress from weaning, transport, and mixing animals from different sources; shared water troughs between pens can spread virus.
- Prevention: quarantine new arrivals for 2 to 3 weeks, vaccinate and deworm before entry (preconditioning), reduce stress. Antibiotics are chosen and dosed by a vet.
(Source: Mwacalimba et al. 2025.)

## Cattle: Trypanosomiasis (nagana) and tsetse

Farmer words: cattle dey thin, dey weak, belly dey go down, tsetse fly dey bite, eyelid pale, hair dey stand.

- Signs: loss of condition, emaciation, anaemia, reduced milk and meat; deaths can reach 50 to 100% within months in exposed susceptible cattle. Tsetse flies carry it.
- Control: tsetse control (traps, insecticide treatment), treatment with trypanocide drugs under vet guidance, and trypanotolerant breeds such as N'Dama. There is no vaccine. Drug resistance is spreading, so use trypanocides only as directed and avoid under-dosing.
(Sources: ILRI and CGIAR trypanosomiasis reviews; see SOURCES.md.)

## Cattle: Tick-borne disease

Tick-borne diseases, including heartwater, are common in cattle in the subhumid zone and take a heavy toll on susceptible animals. Regular tick control (dipping or spraying on a vet-advised schedule) matters, especially for imported or crossbred cattle. This knowledge base has limited detail on specific tick-borne treatments, so recommend a vet visit.
(Source: FAO review of cattle diseases in the subhumid zone.)

## Cattle: Bunaji (White Fulani) performance and feed in northern Nigeria

- Benchmarks from a 12-year on-farm study (1979 to 1990, four subhumid sites): birth weight about 19.6 kg; 76 kg at 9 months; 93 kg at 12 months; age at first calving about 62 months; calving interval about 22.5 months; milk taken for people about 0.5 litre per day (200 to 260 litres per lactation).
- Calf deaths were about 14 to 15% by 9 to 12 months. Calves born in the late wet and early dry season grew and survived best.
- A more recent Oyo State survey found low milk (about 0.6 kg/day), low pregnancy and calving rates, and that low milk, soil-eating and diarrhoea were the commonest problems. About 84% of agropastoralists treated animals themselves with herbs and only about 6% used a vet.
- Dry season (5 to 7 months in the Sudano-Sahelian zone) is the main feed constraint. Supplementing Bunaji cows with cottonseed cake in the dry season has been tested for milk and calf growth.
(Sources: ILRI on-farm Bunaji studies; Livestock Research for Rural Development 21(5). See SOURCES.md.)
